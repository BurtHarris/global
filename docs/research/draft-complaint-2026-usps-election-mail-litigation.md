## NOTE TO COUNSEL (not part of any pleading)

1. **Placeholders.** Every bracketed `[...]` item below is a genuine gap —
   a fact, party, date, or document that has not yet been supplied or
   verified — not a stand-in the drafter has simply forgotten to fill in.
   None should be treated as true or assumed correct.
2. **Confidence levels.** Each numbered paragraph below is tagged:
   - **(C)** = Confirmed against a public record or primary source (see
     the cited footnote, which points back into the Background Report's
     own footnotes).
   - **(U)** = User-asserted, not independently verified.
   - **(P)** = Placeholder; no specific fact has been supplied at all.
3. **Known, serious legal-sufficiency problems.** This draft intentionally
   surfaces — rather than papers over — the same doctrinal problems the
   Background Report already flagged: no identified predicate acts or
   "enterprise" for RICO purposes;[^bg-7] no shown proximate cause running
   from any specific defendant's specific act to a "business or property"
   injury;[^bg-8] no private right of action under RCW ch. 42.52 (Washington
   ethics law is enforced only by the Executive Ethics Board);[^bg-15] and
   vote-dilution claims are ordinarily brought as constitutional or Voting
   Rights Act claims by voters/jurisdictions, not as civil RICO claims.[^bg-1]
   **This document does not resolve any of those problems — it exists so
   counsel can see exactly where they sit inside a complaint's structure.**
4. **No defendant has been served with, or even shown, any version of
   this document.** All characterizations of defendants' conduct are
   either the plaintiff's own unverified allegations or drawn from public
   filings/press by third parties (each footnoted accordingly).

## I. NATURE OF THE ACTION

1. **(P)** This is a civil action for damages and injunctive relief
   arising from an alleged pattern of racketeering activity and related
   misconduct that Plaintiff contends interfered with his business,
   DOGEtech.co, diluted his vote and the votes of similarly situated
   citizens, and was undertaken in part for the partisan advantage of
   certain state officials and political committees, in connection with
   the administration of elections and election mail in Washington State
   and elsewhere.
2. **(U)** Plaintiff does not oppose, and is not adverse to, the United
   States Postal Service or its Ballot Mail for Federal Elections rule;
   to the contrary, Plaintiff supports that rule as an election-security
   measure, and portions of this action are intended to rebut, not
   support, arguments made against USPS by some of the defendants named
   here in a separate, unrelated proceeding.[^bg-part4]

## IV. FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS

### A. The 2026 USPS Ballot Mail Rule and *California v. USPS* (background only — not a claim against USPS)

15. **(C)** On August 26, 2026, the State of California and roughly two
    dozen other states and the District of Columbia sued the United States
    Postal Service and named USPS officials in the District of
    Massachusetts, No. 1:26-cv-13917, challenging USPS's Ballot Mail for
    Federal Elections rule under the Administrative Procedure Act,
    Declaratory Judgment Act, and Privacy Act.[^bg-3]
16. **(C)** That complaint does not name the DNC as a party, does not
    plead civil RICO or qui tam/False Claims Act theories, and seeks only
    declaratory and injunctive relief plus fees and costs — not
    damages.[^bg-5] [^bg-12]
17. **(U)** A Sept. 10, 2026 news report states that a federal judge
    issued a preliminary injunction against the rule on Sept. 4, 2026; this
    has not been independently confirmed against the docket's actual order
    text.[^bg-21]
18. **(U)** Plaintiff contends that the plaintiff states' claims in that
    case regarding compliance costs are a misrepresentation, because
    existing USPS Full-Service Intelligent Mail barcode requirements
    already place barcode-generation burdens on mailers/vendors (such as
    K&H Printers), not on the states, in order to qualify for automation
    rate discounts — **not independently verified against the Domestic
    Mail Manual's text in this research pass.**[^bg-dmm]

### B. The DOJ voter-roll campaign and Washington's involvement

19. **(C)** As of January 28, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice had
    sued twenty-four states and the District of Columbia, including
    **Washington**, over access to state voter-registration
    rolls.[^bg-18]
20. **(C)** Attorney General Pamela Bondi sent a letter dated January 24,
    2026 to Minnesota Governor Tim Walz conditioning an end to ICE/CBP
    deployment partly on voter-roll access; Minnesota's Secretary of State
    publicly characterized this as "an apparent ransom."[^bg-19] **(U)**
    No comparable letter to a Washington official has yet been located or
    supplied.
21. **(C)** In an unrelated case, *United States v. Weber*, No.
    2:25-cv-09149 (C.D. Cal.), the Democratic National Committee appeared
    as a party; that case was dismissed on January 15, 2026.[^bg-20]
22. **(C)** A YouTube video published by the Washington Attorney General's
    official channel shows a January 26, 2026 press conference by Governor
    Bob Ferguson and Attorney General Nick Brown.[^bg-16] **(U)** No
    transcript or specific timestamped statement from that press
    conference establishing any actionable conduct has yet been supplied;
    this is a confirmed-authentic video with an unconfirmed evidentiary
    use.

### C. Washington's ethics-in-public-service law

23. **(C)** RCW ch. 42.52 prohibits Washington state officers and
    employees from using their positions for personal gain, RCW
    § 42.52.020, from receiving special privileges, RCW § 42.52.070, and
    from using public facilities to assist political campaigns, RCW
    § 42.52.180.[^bg-15]
24. **(P)** Enforcement of RCW ch. 42.52 is committed to the Executive
    Ethics Board via administrative complaint, RCW § 42.52.360, and the
    statute does not create a private right of action for damages; no
    specific Washington state officer has yet been identified as having
    violated a specific provision of RCW ch. 42.52 against Plaintiff.[^bg-15]

### D. Plaintiff's alleged injury

25. **(U)** Plaintiff alleges his votes, and those of similarly situated
    citizens, have been diluted. **Vote-dilution theories are ordinarily
    pursued under the Fourteenth Amendment or the Voting Rights Act by
    voters or governmental plaintiffs — not under civil RICO — and no
    specific mechanism of dilution particular to Plaintiff has been
    identified.**
26. **(U)** Plaintiff alleges that unspecified defendants "interfered with"
    DOGEtech.co's ability "to achieve needed success." **No specific act,
    date, defendant, or causal mechanism connecting any named defendant to
    any specific harm suffered by DOGEtech.co has been supplied.** This is
    the single largest evidentiary gap in the entire draft: without it,
    no RICO "business or property" injury and no proximate cause can be
    pleaded as to any defendant.[^bg-8]
27. **(U)** Plaintiff states he has spent over two years independently
    researching Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) violations by
    Democratic candidates. This is Plaintiff's own investigative work
    product, not itself an injury, and FECA violations are enforced by the
    Federal Election Commission administratively (with a limited
    citizen-suit remedy under 52 U.S.C. § 30109(a)(8) only after the FEC
    fails to act on an administrative complaint) — this is not a RICO or
    qui tam predicate by itself.

## V. CAUSES OF ACTION

### COUNT I — Civil RICO, 18 U.S.C. § 1962(c)

28. **(P)** Plaintiff repeats and incorporates ¶¶ 1–27.
29. **(P)** **Missing elements, not yet supplied:** an identified
    "enterprise" distinct from any individual defendant, 18 U.S.C.
    § 1961(4); at least two predicate acts within ten years forming a
    "pattern," 18 U.S.C. § 1961(5), each independently chargeable under
    one of RICO's enumerated predicate statutes (e.g., mail fraud, wire
    fraud, extortion); and proximate cause running from a specific
    predicate act to Plaintiff's specific business-or-property
    injury.[^bg-7] [^bg-8] **None of these elements has yet been supplied
    by Plaintiff for any defendant.** This count cannot presently be
    pleaded in good faith under Fed. R. Civ. P. 11.

### COUNT II — Civil RICO Conspiracy, 18 U.S.C. § 1962(d)

30. **(P)** Plaintiff repeats and incorporates ¶¶ 1–29. Same missing-
    elements problem as Count I, plus an unidentified agreement among two
    or more defendants to participate in the alleged enterprise.

### COUNT III — Qui Tam / False Claims Act, 31 U.S.C. § 3729 *et seq.* (placeholder only)

31. **(P)** No facts suggesting any defendant submitted a false claim for
    payment to the United States government have been identified. **This
    count is included only as a placeholder in case Plaintiff's FEC
    research (¶ 27) develops a specific false-certification theory; it is
    not currently supported and should likely be omitted.**[^bg-2]

### COUNT IV — Violation of RCW ch. 42.52 (placeholder only)

32. **(P)** As noted at ¶ 24, RCW ch. 42.52 is enforced administratively
    and has no private right of action for damages. **This count, as a
    civil damages claim, is not viable as currently understood and would
    need to be reframed — if at all — as an administrative complaint to
    the Executive Ethics Board, not a count in a federal civil
    complaint.**[^bg-15]

### COUNT V — Vote Dilution (placeholder only)

33. **(P)** As noted at ¶ 25, this theory is conventionally a Fourteenth
    Amendment or Voting Rights Act claim, typically brought by voters or
    jurisdictions rather than by an individual alleging business
    injury. **Whether Plaintiff has standing to bring such a claim in his
    individual capacity, and whether it belongs in this action at all
    (as opposed to a separate action), is a threshold question for
    counsel.**
